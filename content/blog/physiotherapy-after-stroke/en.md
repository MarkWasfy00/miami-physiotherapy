A stroke can affect movement, balance, speech and the ability to handle everyday tasks. Its effects differ from person to person depending on where in the brain it happened, how severe it was, how quickly medical care was received and the patient's overall health.

Physiotherapy is a central part of stroke recovery. It helps improve movement, strengthen muscles, train safe walking and transfers, lower the risk of falls and restore as much independence as possible.

Rehab isn't limited to sessions at the center. It needs an integrated plan involving the patient, the family and the medical team, with home exercises, regular follow-up and adjustments as the patient progresses.

> **Important:** Physiotherapy after a stroke should begin only once the patient is medically stable, under the supervision of a doctor and physiotherapist. This article is for awareness and does not replace a medical assessment or an individual rehab plan.

## What is post-stroke physiotherapy?

It's a rehabilitation program that helps the patient regain movement and functions affected by the stroke. It may focus on:

- Improving muscle strength.
- Restoring control of movement.
- Improving balance.
- Training sitting and standing.
- Moving safely from bed to chair.
- Walking safely.
- Using the affected arm or leg.
- Reducing the risk of falls.
- Increasing independence in daily activities.
- Helping the family support the patient safely.

## How a stroke affects movement

- **Weakness on one side of the body** — ranging from mild difficulty to being unable to move a limb normally.
- **Muscle stiffness (spasticity)** — making it harder to move the arm or leg, walk or get dressed.
- **Balance problems** — difficulty staying steady while sitting, standing or walking, which raises the risk of falls.
- **Difficulty walking** — trouble lifting the foot, moving the leg or shifting weight between sides.
- **Poor coordination** — difficulty with hand–eye coordination and fine movements like gripping objects.
- **Fatigue** — tiring after small efforts, so sessions must match the patient's endurance.

## When should physiotherapy start?

Timing depends on the patient's condition, the stability of vital signs and the medical team's assessment. In some cases rehab begins early in hospital once the patient is stable; in others it starts after discharge. It can then continue at a specialist center or at home. Exercises should never be started randomly or forced without guidance, as some movements can be unsuitable or increase the risk of falls.

## How is the patient assessed?

The physiotherapist begins with a comprehensive assessment that may include muscle strength, joint range of motion, stiffness, sitting and standing ability, balance, walking pattern, transfers, control of the affected limb, fatigue and endurance, daily activities, the need for walking aids and home safety. Clear, trackable goals are then set — such as standing for longer, walking a set distance, or moving from bed to chair more safely.

## Goals of rehabilitation

- **Movement and muscle control** — moving the limbs with more control and gradually using affected muscles.
- **Sitting and standing** — maintaining a sitting posture, then moving from sitting to standing safely.
- **Walking** — improving foot placement, weight distribution and arm–leg movement, with an aid if needed.
- **Balance** — improving stability while sitting, standing and walking.
- **Daily activities** — dressing, eating, using the bathroom and moving around the home.
- **Reducing stiffness** — through structured movement and suitable exercise.

## Which exercises help after a stroke?

The right exercises depend on the patient's age, strength and how much movement and balance are affected, so they should be prescribed by a physiotherapist after assessment. They may include:

- **Range-of-motion exercises** to keep joints flexible, assisted by the therapist or a carer if needed.
- **Sitting and trunk-balance exercises**, progressing in difficulty.
- **Transfer and standing practice**, such as bed-to-chair and sit-to-stand, with safety first.
- **Gradual strengthening**, avoiding overexertion or unsuitable weights.
- **Walking practice**, starting with short distances in a safe space.
- **Coordination exercises** for the hands, eyes, limbs and trunk.

Stop any exercise immediately if there is severe pain, dizziness, shortness of breath or unusual fatigue, and inform the doctor or physiotherapist.

## Can rehab be done at home?

Home physiotherapy can suit some patients, especially when getting around is difficult or when training needs to happen in the patient's everyday environment. It can help with moving around the house, assessing fall risks, teaching the family how to help, using a wheelchair or walker, and practising short exercises between sessions. But home rehab doesn't mean random exercises without supervision — it needs a clear plan from a physiotherapist and regular follow-up.

## The family's role

The family plays a big part in recovery, but help should encourage independence rather than make the patient fully dependent. Families can:

- Keep the home safe and remove tripping hazards.
- Make sure hallways are well lit and rugs are secured.
- Help without pulling or yanking the weak arm.
- Encourage the patient to stick with the home exercise program.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a patient walk again after a stroke?

It varies from person to person. Physiotherapy helps train walking gradually and improve balance, strength and control, but no single outcome can be guaranteed for everyone.

### How many sessions are needed?

There's no fixed number. It depends on severity, current ability, goals and how the patient responds. The plan is re-assessed regularly.

### Should the weak limb be moved forcefully?

No. The affected limb should be moved safely and gradually, avoiding forceful pulling — especially at the weak shoulder or arm.

### Can patients still improve long after the stroke?

Some patients continue to improve with training and commitment, even after time has passed. It depends on overall health, current abilities and rehab goals.

## Summary

Physiotherapy is an essential part of stroke rehabilitation and can help improve strength, balance, walking, movement control and daily function. A good plan starts with a thorough assessment, sets gradual goals and tracks progress, with the family helping to create a safe environment.
